Nobody gives you a course on becoming the technical referent. You become it gradually — someone asks you a question, you give a good answer, and it happens again. Then one day you're the person the team points at when architecture decisions get hard, and there's no manual for that.

I've been in that position for a few years now. This is what I've learned about what the role actually requires.

## It's not about knowing everything

The first thing to unlearn is the idea that being the technical referent means having all the answers. It doesn't. What it means is being able to find the right answer faster, ask the right questions, and recognize when a decision is bigger than one person's opinion.

> The most important skill isn't knowing — it's knowing what you don't know, and being comfortable saying it out loud.

Teams lose trust in their referent when they pretend to have certainty they don't have. They gain trust when the referent says "I'm not sure, let me check" and then actually checks.

## Decisions vs. opinions

A lot of technical discussions get stuck because people treat opinions as decisions. As the referent, your job is to separate the two. Some things don't have a right answer — they have tradeoffs. Your job is to surface the tradeoffs clearly so the team can make an informed call, not to impose your preference.

Other things do have a right answer, or at least a clearly better one. In those cases, your job is to move the conversation forward, not extend it. Indecision has a cost that's easy to underestimate.

## The weight of being the reference point

The harder part of the role is psychological. When a production incident happens, people look at you. When an architectural decision turns out to be wrong six months later, you're part of that. You have to be okay with being wrong sometimes, learning from it, and not letting it make you risk-averse.

The referents I've respected most are the ones who take responsibility without drama — who say "this was my call, here's what I missed, here's what we do now." That's the standard I try to hold myself to.

It's a role that grows with you if you let it. The key is to keep being curious, stay close to the code, and never stop being willing to be wrong.
